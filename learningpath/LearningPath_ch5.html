Before you start

In this chapter ANOVA models are treated as linear regression models with dummy regressors, so almost everything builds on what you already know.

  • Dummy regressors for two groups, and the link with the two-sample \(t\)-test (Section 2.9).
  • The multiple linear regression model, the interpretation of its parameters, and inference about a linear combination \(c^t\beta\) (Sections 3.1 and 3.2).
  • Interaction and hierarchical model building (Section 3.2).
  • The decomposition of the total sum of squares and the degrees of freedom (Sections 2.13 and 3.3).
  • The assessment of the model assumptions (Sections 2.8 and 3.6).
  • The diabetes example with its confounder (Section 4.1), and simulation studies of the type I error rate (Section 4.3).

From two groups to several: the one-way ANOVA

Goal: formulate the one-way ANOVA model, see it as a regression model with dummies, and interpret the parameters relative to the reference group.

  1. WatchWatch the video (13 min) on Example (PWD: Post-weaning diarrhea)

    The chapter starts with a new example that will stay with us: a study on post-weaning diarrhea (PWD) in piglets, with five treatments and the average daily weight gain as outcome. Pay attention to the design: the treatments were randomised over 40 pens, and a pen (not a piglet) is the unit of observation. New terminology: a factor, and the levels of a factor.

  2. WatchWatch the video (18 min) on Section 5.1.1: Comparison of Three Means

    The simplest extension of the two-sample problem: the comparison of three means. With two dummy regressors the problem becomes a linear regression model, so that estimation, standard errors and confidence intervals come for free from Chapter 3. It is very important that you can interpret the parameters: the intercept is the mean of the reference group, and every other parameter is a difference with the reference group. Note that R chooses the reference group itself (the first level in alphabetical order), so the same data can give differently looking, but equivalent, output. One thing cannot be done with the \(t\)-tests of Chapter 3: testing that all means (more than two) are equal. That needs the \(F\)-test of the next block.

  3. WatchWatch the video (21 min) on Section 5.1.2: The ANOVA model

    ANOVA has its own terminology and notation. Here you learn the factor effects model \(Y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\), and why it is overparameterised: there are \(t+1\) parameters for \(t\) means. A restriction is needed, and the restriction \(\tau_1 = 0\) brings us back to the dummy coding with a reference group. Make sure that you can translate between the three notations: the \(\beta\)-parameters, the cell means \(\mu_i\) and the factor effects \(\tau_i\).

  4. ReadRead Section 5.1.3: Parameter Estimators

    A short section: the estimators of the ANOVA parameters are simply group means and differences of group means, and their sampling distributions follow from the regression theory.

  5. WatchWatch the video (13 min) on Section 5.1.4: Sum of Squares

    The sums of squares are the same as for regression, but with ANOVA names and notation: SSR becomes SST, the treatment sum of squares (or between sum of squares). Understand what it measures: the variability of the group means about the overall mean. MSE is now the pooled variance estimator.

  6. AIWhich group is the reference?

    Fit the one-way ANOVA model for ADWG0050 with all five treatments, as in the example of Section 5.2. Give only the coefficient table of the summary output to the AI assistant, and ask for the interpretation of the coefficient of TreatmentD. Does the answer say with which group treatment D is compared? Does it state this as a fact or as an assumption? Then change the reference group to B, as in Section 5.3.1, give the new output, and ask whether the two analyses contradict each other. A good answer explains that the model is the same and only the parameterisation differs.

  7. AIDummy codings without a reference group.

    Consider again a one-way ANOVA with a factor with 5 levels. Ask AI to give you two different dummy codings that do not involve a reference group. Together with AI as a discussion partner, come to clear interpretations of the model parameters for these two alternative dummy coding systems.

  8. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • What is a factor, what are its levels, and what is the reference group?
    • For three groups, define the two dummies and give the interpretation of \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\).
    • Convince yourself that e.g. in a model with two dummies (factor with three levels), the interpretation of a regression parameter does not require to keep the other dummy (=regressor) constant.
    • Write the one-way ANOVA model as a cell means model and as a factor effects model. Why is the latter overparameterised, and how is this solved?
    • How does R choose the reference group, and how does this affect the output?
    • Express the estimators \(\hat\mu\) and \(\hat\tau_i\) in terms of sample means.
    • What does SST measure, and what are the degrees of freedom of SST and SSE in a one-way ANOVA with \(t\) groups?
    • Why is MSE called the pooled variance estimator?

The F-test and contrasts

Goal: test hypotheses about several parameters at once with the \(F\)-test, and estimate and test contrasts between group means.

  1. WatchWatch the video (28 min) on Section 5.2: The \(F\)-test

    The null hypothesis that all means are equal involves several \(\beta\)-parameters at once, and for this you need a new test: the \(F\)-test. It is introduced for the general linear hypothesis \(H_0: A\beta = a\), which covers many more situations than the comparison of all means. The construction follows three steps (the restricted estimator, the restricted SSE and the test statistic), with the results given without proof. For the one-way ANOVA the statistic reduces to MST/MSE. Remember what a significant \(F\)-test tells you, and what not: at least two means differ, but not which ones.

  2. DoMake Exercise: PWD - FCR

    An analysis of the food conversion rate. You need the \(F\)-test for the treatment effect, and the interpretation and confidence interval of one parameter. Think about the reference group before you interpret.

  3. ReadRead Section 5.3.1: Setting the reference level, Section 5.3.2: Examples of other contrasts

    There is no video recording available. First you learn how to choose the reference group yourself, so that the parameters answer the research question (here: comparing all treatments with the standard feed B). Note that the \(F\)-test does not change. Then you learn to estimate other contrasts, such as the vaccinated groups versus the non-vaccinated groups, as a linear combination \(A\beta\), with its standard error, confidence interval and test. This is the technique of the blood pressure exercise of Section 3.2 again. Also note the warning at the end of Section 5.3.1: looking at several \(p\)-values at once is not allowed without a correction; this is the topic of Section 5.6.

  4. AIAI as assistant for a contrast

    You want to compare the vaccinated groups (C, D and E) with the non-vaccinated groups (A and B) for ADWG0050. First write down the contrast yourself, in terms of the means and then in terms of the \(\beta\)-parameters, for reference group A. Then ask the AI assistant for R code that gives the estimate, the standard error, the 95% confidence interval and the \(p\)-value of this contrast. Check three things: the vector it uses (it should be equivalent to \((0, -1/2, 1/3, 1/3, 1/3)\)), the reference group it assumes, and the result, which should agree with the notes (an estimate of 36.6 g/day with SE 9.7, and an interval from 16.8 to 56.4). If its numbers differ, find the cause before you decide who is right.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Write the null hypothesis of equal means for three groups as a general linear hypothesis: give \(A\) and \(a\).
    • What are the restricted estimator and the restricted SSE, and why is \(\text{SSE}_0 \geq \text{SSE}\)?
    • Give the \(F\)-test statistic for the general linear hypothesis and its null distribution. What are \(m\) and \(p\)?
    • What is the relation between the \(F\)-test and the \(t\)-test when two means are compared?
    • Why does the \(F\)-test for the treatment effect not change when another reference group is chosen?
    • What is a contrast? Write the comparison of vaccinated versus non-vaccinated piglets as \(A\beta\).
    • When can a contrast be tested with a \(t\)-test instead of an \(F\)-test?

The two-way ANOVA

Goal: analyse an outcome as a function of two factors, with and without interaction, and test the effects with \(F\)-tests in the right order.

  1. WatchWatch the video (21 min) on Section 5.4.1: The additive model

    A second factor is added: besides treatment also the gender of the piglets. In the additive model the effect of one factor is the same at every level of the other factor. There is a sum of squares for each factor, and an \(F\)-test for each main effect. An important remark: in general SSTot is not equal to SST + SSA + SSE; this decomposition only holds for orthogonal designs, such as the balanced PWD study. Note also that the example uses the Anova function of the car package and not the anova function of base R; the difference is explained in Section 5.5.3.

  2. DoMake Exercise: interpretation of the parameters

    A short theoretical exercise: show from the model that in the additive model the effects of one factor do not depend on the level of the other factor.

  3. WatchWatch the video (12 min) on Section 5.4.4: The non-additive ANOVA model

    The non-additive model, with interaction effects \((\tau\alpha)_{ij}\). As in Section 3.2, the model is formulated as a regression model by multiplying dummies, the main effects lose their clear interpretation when interaction is present, and the hierarchical approach applies: look at the interaction first. You also learn to read an interaction plot: parallel lines suggest that there is no interaction.

  4. DoMake Exercise: interpretation of the parameters, Exercise: degrees of freedom

    Two short exercises: the interpretation of the parameters in the model with interaction, and where the degrees of freedom \(n - ta\) come from. Counting parameters is a skill that you need for every ANOVA table.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Write down the additive two-way ANOVA model with its restrictions. How many parameters are estimated?
    • What do SST and SSA measure, and what are their degrees of freedom?
    • When does SSTot = SST + SSA + SSE hold, and how can you check this for a dataset?
    • Write down the non-additive model. How many interaction parameters are there after the restrictions?
    • How are the dummies for the interaction constructed?
    • Give the \(F\)-test statistic for the interaction and its null distribution.
    • In the PWD example the interaction is not significant (\(p = 0.293\)). What do you do next, and why?
    • How do you read an interaction plot?

Extra sum of squares and the types of sum of squares

Goal: understand the \(F\)-test as a comparison of two nested models, and know which hypotheses are tested by type I, II and III sums of squares.

  1. ReadRead Section 5.5.1: The \(F\)-test in a one-way ANOVA, Section 5.5.2: An \(F\)-test for nested models

    There is no video recording available. The \(F\)-test is constructed in another way: as a comparison of a model with and a model without certain terms. The extra sum of squares is the increase in SSE when the terms are left out. This gives an \(F\)-test for any two nested models, which is very useful in practice: in the example it tests in one step whether gender has any effect at all, main effect and interaction together. Note the discussion of why the test statistic is divided by the MSE of the larger model.

  2. ReadRead Section 5.5.3: types of SS

    There is no video recording available. Software reports different types of sums of squares, and they test different hypotheses. Type I (sequential) depends on the order in which the terms are entered. Type III tests each term given all other terms, which is not meaningful for a main effect when the interaction is in the model. Type II respects the hierarchy. Study the diabetes example carefully: the same data give a very significant or a completely non-significant treatment effect, depending on the order of the terms, and you already know from Chapter 4 which of the two answers the research question. In R: anova gives type I, and Anova of the car package gives type II or III.

  3. AIWhich ANOVA table did you get?

    Fit the two-way model for the diabetes data (condition, treatment and their interaction) and ask the AI assistant: "Give me R code for the ANOVA table of this model, and tell me whether the treatment has an effect." Which type of sum of squares does its code produce? Does it tell you, and does it explain which hypothesis each line tests? Now ask for the same analysis with the two factors entered in the other order and see whether it notices that the conclusion about treatment changes. Finally, state the research question (the effect of treatment, with condition as a confounder that must stay in the model) and ask which line of which table answers it. Compare with the notes of Section 5.5.3.

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Define the extra sum of squares for two nested models. What are its degrees of freedom?
    • Give the \(F\)-test statistic for nested models and its null distribution. Why is it divided by \(\text{MSE}_1\) and not by \(\text{MSE}_0\)?
    • Show that the \(F\)-test of the one-way ANOVA is a special case.
    • What is tested on each line of a type I ANOVA table? Why does the order of the terms matter?
    • What is tested with type III sums of squares, and why can the tests for main effects not be interpreted when an interaction is in the model?
    • How do type II sums of squares differ from type III?
    • In the diabetes example, which analysis gives the test for the treatment effect that answers the research question?

Multiple comparisons of means

Goal: understand the multiplicity problem, the familywise error rate, and the methods that control it.

  1. WatchWatch the video (14 min) on Section 5.6.1: Formulation of the multiplicity problem

    A significant \(F\)-test says that not all means are equal, but not which ones differ. The naive solution is to test all pairs of means with \(t\)-tests at the 5% level. A simulation study shows what goes wrong: with five groups, and thus ten comparisons, the probability of at least one false rejection is about 27% instead of 5%. This is the multiplicity problem. The video continues with Section 5.6.2, in which the type I error rate is generalised to the familywise error rate (FWER): the probability of at least one false positive result among all tests. You also learn the notions of per-comparison significance level and adjusted \(p\)-value.

  2. WatchWatch the video (26 min) on Section 5.6.3: The Bonferroni method

    The Bonferroni method is the simplest method to control the FWER: test each of the \(m\) hypotheses at the level \(\alpha/m\), or multiply the \(p\)-values by \(m\). It follows from a simple probability inequality, and it is conservative, more so when \(m\) is large. This is again demonstrated with a simulation study. The video ends with the method of Sidak. The recording stops after that, so the remaining methods are for reading.

  3. ReadRead Section 5.6.4: The Holm-Bonferroni method, Section 5.6.5: The Tukey-Kramer method, Section 5.6.6: Simultaneous CI

    There is no video recording available. The Holm-Bonferroni method is a stepwise improvement of Bonferroni and more powerful. The Tukey-Kramer method is developed specifically for all pairwise comparisons of means, is based on the distribution of the largest test statistic, and is not conservative. Compare the results for the PWD example: Tukey finds three significant differences where (Holm-)Bonferroni finds one. Finally, simultaneous confidence intervals are the counterpart of FWER control, just as a confidence interval is the counterpart of a single test.

  4. DoMake Exercise: equivalence simultaneous CI and FWER control

    A simulation study that you set up yourself; for this exercise there is no solution in the course notes. Use the simulation studies of Sections 5.6.1 and 5.6.3 as a starting point.

  5. AIThe AI does the analysis, you audit it

    In this chapter you let the AI assistant do a larger piece of work, and your role becomes that of an auditor. Ask it: "Compare all pairs of treatments for ADWG0050 in the PWD data and tell me which treatments differ." Then check its work with this list. Did it correct for multiplicity, and with which method? Did it say at which FWER level? Did it use the MSE of the ANOVA model for all comparisons, or a separate variance for every pair? Do the adjusted \(p\)-values agree with the notes (with Tukey: 0.021 for E versus B, 0.044 for D versus B and 0.049 for C versus B; with Bonferroni only E versus B is significant, with 0.027)? Does its conclusion name the treatments and give effect sizes with their uncertainty, or only "significant" and "not significant"? Write in your logbook which points of this list the AI got right without being asked.

  6. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Explain the multiplicity problem. How many pairwise comparisons are there for \(t\) groups?
    • Why is it better to use the MSE of the ANOVA model than a separate pooled variance for every pair of groups?
    • Define the familywise error rate. To what does it reduce for a single test?
    • What are a per-comparison significance level and an adjusted \(p\)-value?
    • Derive the Bonferroni method. Why is it conservative?
    • On which assumption is the method of Sidak based, and why is this assumption often not realistic?
    • Describe the Holm-Bonferroni procedure. Why is it more powerful than Bonferroni?
    • On which test statistic is the Tukey-Kramer method based, and for which situation is it developed?
    • What are simultaneous confidence intervals, and how are they related to FWER control?

Assessing the model assumptions, and a complete analysis

Goal: assess the assumptions of an ANOVA model, and carry out and report a complete two-way analysis.

  1. ReadRead Section 5.7: Assessment of model assumptions

    There is no video recording available. Since every ANOVA model is a linear regression model, the assessment proceeds as before, with one new insight: a model with all main effects and interactions has a parameter for every combination of factor levels, so it imposes no restriction on the means and the conditional mean model does not need to be assessed; the additive model does impose restrictions. Note how carefully the plots are interpreted when there are only a few replicates per group: with four observations per combination, a boxplot cannot show much.

  2. AIA complete analysis with AI, and looking back

    Ask the AI assistant for a complete analysis of ADWG0050 with the factors Treatment and Sex, from the choice of the model to the conclusions, and audit it against the course notes. Did it start with the interaction (the notes find \(p = 0.293\)) and refit the additive model before interpreting the main effects (treatment \(p = 0.008\), gender \(p = 0.105\))? Which type of sum of squares did it use, and does it know? Did it assess the assumptions, and did it take into account that there are only four replicates per combination? Did it handle the comparisons between treatments with a multiplicity correction? Are the conclusions formulated in terms of piglets, feed and weight gain, with effect sizes and their imprecision? Then read through your AI logbook for the whole course. You started by letting the AI explain and by checking it; you now let it do an analysis and audit it. Write down your own checklist for auditing an AI-assisted analysis; you will use it for the report in Chapter 6.

  3. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Why is there no need to assess the conditional mean model in a two-way ANOVA with interaction? And in the additive model?
    • Which plots do you use to assess the constant variance assumption in an ANOVA model?
    • What is the difficulty of assessing the assumptions with only four replicates per combination of factor levels?
    • Give the steps of a complete two-way ANOVA, in the right order, from the model with interaction to the conclusions.
