Chapter 6: Reporting

Work through the steps in order. Each step links to the online course notes. Tick a step when you have finished it; your ticks are remembered in this browser.

Before you start

This chapter stands on its own: you can read the guidelines at any time during the course, and it is a good idea to do so early. The tasks below, however, ask you to write about analyses from the earlier chapters.

  • For the first two blocks: Chapter 2 is sufficient.
  • For the last block: the analyses of Chapters 3 to 5, and the checklist for auditing an AI-assisted analysis that you wrote at the end of Chapter 5.
  • The rules of this course on the use of AI: The use of AI in Linear Models.

The guidelines

Goal: know the guidelines for reporting the results of a statistical analysis, and the reason behind each of them.

  1. ReadRead Chapter 6: Reporting

    There is no video recording available. The chapter is a list of twelve guidelines, and it is short. Do not only memorise them: for each guideline, ask yourself what could go wrong for the reader of the report if it is not followed. Three of them deserve special attention, because they are about what a statistician may and may not do. (1) The study design is always reported, because it determines which methods are valid and whether a causal conclusion is allowed (recall Section 2.10 and Chapter 4). (2) Observations are never removed to make the data fit the method; strange observations are identified and reported. (3) A hypothesis test is seldom reported on its own: the estimate and its confidence interval belong with it. Also note the very precise advice on how to write a confidence interval, with the word "to" and with units.

  2. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): the guidelines in action. Take the solution of one exercise that you made earlier, for example the blood pressure exercise of Section 2.6 or the lead concentration exercise of Section 3.1, and go through the twelve guidelines one by one. Which ones are followed in the conclusions of that solution? Which ones do not apply, because the solution is written for statisticians and not for a client? Look in particular at the last guideline and its exception.

  3. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Why must the study design always be reported? Give two things that depend on it.
    • What should a statistician do with an unexpected observation, and what not?
    • You estimate an effect as 1.79 with a 95% confidence interval that stretches 0.35 to either side, in mmHg per mg/day. Write this down in the recommended way, and give two ways in which it should not be written.
    • What has to be mentioned when the result of a hypothesis test is reported, and what should accompany it?
    • What do you report when the results do not allow you to answer the research question?
    • When may software output be shown in a report, and where?

Practice: from output to report

Goal: recognise poor reporting, and write a short report that follows the guidelines.

  1. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): repair a bad report. The following conclusion was written for the blood pressure example, by someone who had not read Chapter 6: "We ran a regression in R. Coefficients: dose 1.79, p < 0.05. The effect is significant, so the drug works. beta1 = 1.79 ± 0.35. One patient looked like an outlier and was removed before the analysis." List every guideline that is violated; there are at least eight. Then rewrite the conclusion so that it follows the guidelines. You will notice that you cannot do this without information that is missing from the text; write down which information you had to look up in the course notes.

  2. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): write your own report. Choose one analysis from the course, for example the effect of the blood lead concentration on the finger-wrist tapping score (Chapter 3) or the effect of the treatments on the weight gain of the piglets (Chapter 5). Write a report of at most one page for the researcher who collected the data, not for a statistician. A structure that works: the study design and the research question; what the data exploration showed; the model and why it was chosen; the results, with estimates, confidence intervals and tests; the answer to the research question; and the software output in an appendix. Write it without AI. Then put it away for a day and read it again as if you were the researcher.

  3. AIAI as reviewer of your report

    Give your report to the AI assistant, together with the guidelines of Chapter 6, and ask it to review the report against the guidelines, one guideline at a time, without rewriting anything. Judge every remark: is it correct, is it relevant, or is the AI asking for something that is not in the guidelines? Then ask a second question: "Which conclusion in this report is stronger than the analysis allows?" Check its answer against what you know about the study design and about non-significant results. Revise the report yourself. Note in your logbook which of the AI's remarks you accepted, which you rejected, and why.

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Give the guidelines that are violated in the bad report of this block.
    • For the analysis that you reported: what is the study design, and which kind of conclusion does it allow?
    • Did you answer the research question in the terminology of the application? Read your last paragraph aloud to check.
    • Which numbers in your report have no unit, and should they have one?

An AI-assisted analysis and report

Goal: carry out and report a complete data analysis with the help of an AI assistant, remain responsible for all of it, and be transparent about the role of the AI.

  1. Note

    This is where the AI steps of the whole learning path come together. In Chapters 1 and 2 the AI explained and you checked. In Chapter 3 it assisted with parts of the work. In Chapter 4 you learned that it does not know the design of your study. In Chapter 5 you audited its analysis. Now you do what you will do as a statistician: a complete analysis and report, with AI as an assistant and with you as the responsible author.

  2. AIA complete analysis and report, with an AI statement

    Choose a dataset and a research question from the course (or exercises, ...) that you have not yet reported on. Carry out the analysis and write the report, and use the AI assistant wherever you think it helps: for R code, for checking your reasoning, for improving the wording. Three conditions apply. (1) Before you start, give the AI the information that it cannot know: the design, the research question, the meaning and the units of the variables. (2) Audit everything it produces with the checklist that you wrote at the end of Chapter 5. (3) Add a short AI statement at the end of the report: for which parts you used AI, what it produced, how you verified it, and what you changed or rejected. This statement is what the rules of this course and of the university ask from you: you acknowledge the use of AI, and you remain accountable.

  3. Note

    Try it yourself: the test without AI. Put the report next to you and close the AI assistant. For every sentence in the results and the conclusion, ask yourself: can I explain where this comes from, and could I have written it myself? For every piece of R code: can I explain each line? Whatever you cannot explain, is not yet yours; go back to the course notes for those parts. This is also the best preparation for the exam, where you will not have AI.

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Which information did the AI need from you before it could help with the analysis?
    • Which errors or weak points did your audit find, and how did you find them?
    • Does your AI statement allow a reader to see exactly what the AI contributed and how you verified it?
    • Is there any sentence or any line of code in your report that you cannot explain without AI?
    • Looking back at your AI logbook for the whole course: what are your own rules for using AI in a data analysis?

After this chapter you can

  • write a report of a statistical analysis that follows the guidelines: design, research question, data exploration, results with units, confidence intervals and tests, and an answer to the research question;
  • formulate conclusions precisely and with appropriate nuance, for a reader who is not a statistician;
  • recognise and repair poor reporting;
  • use an AI assistant as a reviewer and as an assistant in a complete analysis, audit its contribution, and report its role transparently;
  • explain and defend every part of an AI-assisted analysis without AI.
