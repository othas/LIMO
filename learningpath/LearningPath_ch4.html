This chapter has little new mathematics, but it asks you to think carefully about how the data came about. You will need the following.

  • The basics of causal inference: counterfactuals, consistency and mean exchangeability, and why randomisation helps (Section 2.10).
  • Dummy regressors (Section 2.9).
  • Hypothesis tests: type I error, power, and the null distribution of the test statistic (Section 2.7).

Confounding and causal diagrams

Goal: recognise a confounder, draw a causal diagram, and understand why a confounder must be included in the model.

  1. WatchWatch the video (18 min) on Section 4.1.1: Causal diagrams and controling for confounders

    We return to the weird conclusion of Chapter 3: the longer children lived near the lead smelter, the better their score. Age explains it: age affects both the outcome and the regressor. Such a variable is a confounder. You learn to visualise the relations between the variables in a causal diagram (causal graph). It is very important to understand that the arrows in a causal diagram are not derived from the data; they come from reasoning and prior knowledge. The data analysis only estimates the sizes of the effects. You also learn the difference between a marginal association and a conditional association (given the confounder), and that the additive regression model gives you the conditional one.

  2. WatchWatch the video (12 min) on Example (Diabetes)

    A second example: a clinical study in which the clinician decided who received the new drug. Ignoring the general condition of the patients, the drug seems to work very well; when the condition is included in the model, the treatment effect vanishes completely. Make sure that you can explain, with the causal diagram, why the first analysis is misleading. At the end a question is asked about a properly randomised version of the study; keep it in mind, the answer comes in Section 4.2.

  3. AIWhat the AI cannot know

    Fit the two models of the diabetes example yourself (with and without condition). Give both outputs to the AI assistant, at first without any explanation of how the study was done, and ask: "Which of these two analyses gives the effect of the treatment?" Does it choose? Does it ask how the patients were assigned to the treatments? Then tell it that the clinician chose the treatment on the basis of the patient's condition, and ask again. Finally tell it instead that the treatment was randomised, and compare its answer with the last paragraph of the example. Note in your logbook: which information decided the answer, and was that information in the data?

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • What makes a variable a confounder? Give the two relations that it must have.
    • Draw the causal diagram of the lead concentration example. Where do the arrows come from?
    • What is the difference between the marginal and the conditional association between Totyrs and MAXFWT, and which regression model estimates which?
    • In the diabetes example, why does the treatment seem to work when condition is ignored?
    • If the diabetes study had been randomised, would condition still be a confounder? Why?

Causal effects, unmeasured confounders and colliders

Goal: know under which conditions a regression parameter is a causal effect, and why you should not control for a collider.

  1. WatchWatch the video (8 min) on Section 4.1.2: Causality and confounders

    A short but important section. A causal effect can be estimated without bias if the confounders are in the model, the model is correctly specified, and consistency and conditional mean exchangeability hold; compare this with Section 2.10, where the conditions were stated without confounders. This sounds promising, but there is a catch: what if not all confounders are measured, or even known? With observational data you can only claim a causal effect if you are very confident that all confounders are in the model.

  2. WatchWatch the video (23 min) on Section 4.1.3: Colliders

    After confounders you might think that adding more regressors is always the safe choice. The study time example shows that it is not. The school result is affected by both the study time and the educational level of the mother: it is a collider. Controlling for a collider creates an association that is not causal (spurious correlation). The simulation study at the end demonstrates this, again with the repeated sampling reasoning that you know from Chapter 2.

  3. AIDoes the AI follow your arrows?

    Describe the study time example to the AI assistant (outcome study time, regressor educational level of the mother, and the school result as an extra variable) and ask: "Should I control for the school result?" Look at how it reasons. Does it say that controlling for more variables is always better? Does it ask about, or assume, the direction of the arrows? Now tell it, as if you were sure, that the school result influences the study time and not the other way round, and ask again. If the advice changes, you have seen the main message of this section: the causal assumptions come from you, not from the data and not from the AI, and the analysis is only as good as those assumptions.

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Under which conditions can a causal effect be estimated without bias from observational data?
    • What is conditional mean exchangeability, and how does it differ from mean exchangeability in Section 2.10?
    • What is an unmeasured confounder, and why is it a problem that no analysis can repair?
    • What is a collider? Draw the causal diagram of the study time example.
    • Explain the difference between a confounder and a collider in terms of the directions of the arrows.
    • What goes wrong when you control for a collider, and how does the simulation study show this?

Collapsibility

Goal: understand when the effect of the target regressor is the same with and without a covariate in the model, and what is gained by including the covariate.

  1. WatchWatch the video (44 min) on Section 4.2: Collapsibility

    In the randomised blood pressure study, age is neither a confounder nor a collider. So may you include it or not? Collapsibility gives the answer: the effect of the target regressor \(X\) is the same in the model with and without the covariate \(Z\) if \(Z\) is independent of \(X\), or if \(Y\) and \(Z\) are conditionally independent given \(X\). In a randomised study the first condition holds by design. Both models then give unbiased estimators of the same causal effect, but including a covariate that is correlated with the outcome gives a smaller standard error. Note the warning at the end: you may not fit both models and then pick the one with the nicest result. Important: the video was recorded with an earlier version of this section. The conclusions and the example are the same, but the derivation in the current course notes (Scenario 1 and Scenario 2) is different from the one in the video. Study the derivation from the course notes.

  2. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): repeat the analysis of the blood pressure example, and compute the correlation between age and the outcome within each treatment group. Then explain in your own words why the standard error of the treatment effect is so much smaller in the model with age (0.86 versus 1.65 mmHg), although both estimators are unbiased for the same effect.

  3. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Write down the two models \(M_x\) and \(M_{xz}\). What does it mean that the effect of \(X\) is collapsible over \(Z\)?
    • Give the two conditions under which collapsibility holds, and derive the result for one of them.
    • Which of the two conditions holds in a randomised study, and why?
    • In the blood pressure example the two estimates of the treatment effect are numerically different (5.4 and 5.8). Does this contradict collapsibility?
    • When does adding a covariate reduce the standard error of the effect of interest?
    • May you interpret the treatment effect in the model with age as a marginal effect? Why?
    • Answer the question at the end of the diabetes example: in the randomised version, are you allowed to include condition in the model?

Randomisation restriction

Goal: understand why a stratification (blocking) factor must be included in the model.

  1. WatchWatch the video (18 min) on Section 4.3: Randomisation restriction

    Here treatments are randomised within groups (for example within men and within women), so that the design is balanced. Such a factor is called a stratification factor or blocking factor. It is not a confounder and the treatment effect is collapsible over it, so you might think that you are free to leave it out. You are not: because it restricts the randomisation, it must be in the model, even if its effect is not significant. The issue is not bias, but the validity of the inference: standard errors, confidence intervals and tests. This is not proven but demonstrated with a simulation study under the null hypothesis; make sure that you understand why the p-values should be uniformly distributed and what the empirical type I error rate tells you.

  2. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): run the simulation study of this section, and then repeat it with the effect of gender set to zero in the data generating model. What happens now to the type I error rate of the analysis that ignores gender? Explain what you see. Then make the effect of gender larger than in the notes and look again.

  3. AIA non-significant blocking factor

    Ask the AI assistant: "I ran a trial in which treatment was randomised separately within men and within women, ten and ten in each. In my model the effect of gender is not significant, p = 0.6. Can I remove gender from the model to keep it simple?" Compare the answer with the conclusion of Section 4.3. If the AI agrees to remove it, ask what happens to the standard error and the type I error rate of the treatment effect, and check its claims with the simulation study that you ran yourself. If it says that gender must stay, ask for its reason and check whether it is the reason given in the notes (validity of the inference) or another one (for example bias).

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • What is a randomisation restriction? Explain with the example why fewer treatment assignments are possible.
    • What are a stratum and a blocking factor?
    • Is gender a confounder in this design? Is the treatment effect collapsible over gender?
    • What goes wrong when the blocking factor is left out of the model: the estimator of the treatment effect, its standard error, or the type I error rate?
    • Why is a non-significant effect of the blocking factor no reason to remove it?
    • Which interpretation, marginal or conditional, does the treatment effect have in the model with the blocking factor?

Sample size and power

Goal: derive the power of the test for a regression parameter, and use it to design a study.

  1. WatchWatch the video (19 min) on Section 4.4: Sample size and power

    The power of a test is the probability to reject the null hypothesis when a specific alternative, \(\beta_1 = \delta\), is true. To find it you need the distribution of the test statistic under that alternative. With known \(\sigma^2\) this is a normal distribution shifted by the noncentrality parameter \(\delta/\sigma_{\beta_1}\); with estimated \(\sigma^2\) it becomes a noncentral \(t\)-distribution (given without proof). The key insight for designing studies: the power depends on the design through \(\sigma_{\beta_1}\), and thus through the whole design matrix \(X\), not only through the sample size.

  2. WatchWatch the video (26 min) on Example (two 0/1 dummies)

    A study with two treatments in two hospitals has to be designed so that a treatment effect of 2 is detected with 80% power. In R, a function for the design matrix and a function for the power are written, and the power is plotted against the sample size. Follow the code line by line: you should be able to write such functions yourself for another design.

  3. Note

    Try it yourself (not in the course notes): use the R functions of the example to answer three new questions. What sample size is needed for a power of 90%? What if the residual variance is 5 instead of 3? And what happens to the power for \(n = 28\) if the design is not balanced, for example with three times as many patients in one hospital as in the other?

  4. AIAI as assistant for a sample size calculation, and looking back

    Ask the AI assistant for the sample size of the example, in words: a two-sided test at the 5% level, 80% power, a treatment effect of 2, a residual variance of 3, two treatments in two hospitals, hospital included in the model, a balanced design. Compare its answer with the \(n = 28\) of the notes. If the numbers differ, find out why before deciding who is right: is its number per group or in total? Did it use a normal or a \(t\)-distribution? Did it account for the hospital in the model? Did it round to a multiple of four? Ask it for R code and run it. Finally, read through your AI logbook for this chapter. More than in the previous chapters, the right answer depended on information about the design that only you could provide. Write down which questions about the design you will from now on always answer before you start an analysis, with or without AI.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Give the definition of the power of a test. Why is it only defined for a specific alternative?
    • What is the power when \(\delta = 0\)?
    • Derive the distribution of the test statistic under the alternative when \(\sigma^2\) is known.
    • What is the noncentrality parameter, and which three things make it larger?
    • What changes when \(\sigma^2\) is estimated?

    • In the example, what is meant by the smallest clinically relevant effect size, and why does the choice of \(\sigma^2 = 3\) matter?
