Properties of the least squares estimator

Goal: understand the sampling distribution of the LSE and its interpretation in terms of repeated sampling.

  1. Note

    Section 2.3 is about the properties of the least squares estimator. It is important to realise that the LSE \(\hat\beta\) is a random variable (we start from randomly sampled data and since the LSE is a function of the data, the LSE is also a random variable). The distribution of \(\hat\beta\) is referred to as the sampling distribution. These properties are very, very important. It is thanks to these theoretical properties that we know that the LSE is a good estimator to be used in real data analysis. The properties are in the first place expressed in mathematical language/notation, but we will also give an interpretation in terms of repeated sampling. It is very important that you understand the concept of repeated sampling. It will be very often used throughout the course and it gives the so-called frequentist interpretation of statistical analyses (in contrast to the Bayesian interpretation that you will learn in other courses).

  2. WatchWatch the video (30 min) on Section 2.3.1: Mean and variance of the LSE

    Here you learn that the LSE is unbiased and that its variance is a measure for imprecision.

  3. DoMake Exercise: simulation study

    You are asked to set up a simulation study for empirically confirming the unbiasedness of the LSE and confirm the expression of the variance. Make sure that you understand the simulation study very well. It gives you a realistic and practically relevant interpretation of concepts as the mean and the variance. Later simulation studies make use of the same concepts and algorithm.

  5. WatchWatch the video (7 min) on Section 2.3.2: Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE)

    Here we demonstrate that the LSE \(\hat\beta\) is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE). It is a so-called optimality property and forms a central argument to advise data analysts to use the LSE for estimating parameters in linear models (i.e. there are other types of estimators, but in terms of bias and variance, the LSE is the best).

  6. DoMake Exercise: Simulation study

    Again a simulation study.

  7. WatchWatch the video (26 min) on Section 2.3.3: Sampling distribution of the LSE

    Whereas in the previous two sections only the mean and the variance of the LSE \(\hat\beta\) were studied, we study here the sampling distribution of the estimator. However, this is only possible if we assume a particular distribution for the error term \(\varepsilon\) (or, equivalently, we assume a particular distribution for the conditional distribution of \(Y\), given \(x\)). Note that the mean and variance of \(\hat\beta\) do not require such a distributional assumption (i.e. they can be studied in the semiparametric model), but for finding the sampling distribution of \(\hat\beta\) we need a distributional assumption (making the model a parametric model). The reason why we can only find the distribution of \(\hat\beta\) if the (conditional) distribution of \(Y\) is known is simple: \(\hat\beta\) is a function of \(Y\) and hence the distribution of the estimator is governed by the distribution of the data. You learn that \(\hat\beta\) has a normal distribution (if \(\varepsilon\) is normal); see Theorem 2.4. Theorem 2.5 gives the asymptotic sampling distribution. This theorem basically says that in large samples the LSE is approximately normally distributed, even without the normality assumption (i.e. in the semiparametric model). Why are we interested in the sampling distribution? Well, it is needed for the construction of confidence intervals (CI) and hypothesis tests. Here is one way of looking at it: CIs and hypothesis tests come with probabilistic expressions (e.g. a confidence level of 95%, and a significance level of 5%). Probabilities can be derived from distributions; in our case we will need the sampling distribution of the estimator.

    You do not have to learn this section, but it may be interesting to read it if you are familiar with maximum likelihood estimation (which is introduced in the course Concepts of Probability and Statistics).

The residual variance and the studentised estimator

Goal: estimate \(\sigma^2\) and know the distribution of the studentised estimator, the basis for confidence intervals and tests.

  1. WatchWatch the video (7 min) on Section 2.4: An Estimator of \(\sigma^2\)

    The previous section focused on the estimation of the \(\beta\)-parameters in the model. There is one more model parameter that needs to be estimated: the residual variance \(\sigma^2\). You will learn that the minimised least squares criterion (SSE) divided by \(n-2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\), even in the semiparametric model: \(\text{SSE}/(n-2) = \text{MSE} = \hat\sigma^2\). If also normality is assumed, Theorem 2.7 tells us that the sampling distribution of \(\hat\sigma^2\) is related to a chi-squared distribution. We will need this result later for the construction of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests about the \(\beta\)-parameters.

  2. DoMake Exercise: Simulation study

    It is again a simulation study.

  3. WatchWatch the video (16 min) on Section 2.5: Sampling Distributions of the Standardised and the Studentised LSE

    It's a very short and easy section, though not less important; perhaps it is enough to read through this section instead of watching the video. Here the sampling distributions of the standardised and studentised parameter estimators are derived. The former makes use of the true variance of the estimator, and the latter makes use of the estimated variance (involving MSE). It's this studentised estimator that will form the basis of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. Also note the distinction between small sample results for which the normality assumption is required, and the large sample (asymptotic) results which also hold in the semiparametric model.

  4. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Why do we divide SSE by \(n-2\) and not by \(n\)?
    • What is the difference between the standardised and the studentised estimator, and which one can be used in practice?
    • Which distribution does the studentised estimator have under normality? And in large samples without the normality assumption?

Goal: construct and interpret confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for a \(\beta\)-parameter, and formulate conclusions precisely.

  1. WatchWatch the video (35 min) on Section 2.6: Confidence Intervals

    Here the confidence intervals of the \(\beta\)-parameters are developed, starting from the studentised estimators. The mathematical development is very straightforward. It is particularly important to understand the repeated sampling interpretation of a confidence interval; this interpretation is best understood by looking at the simulation studies in this section.

  2. DoMake Exercise: Blood Pressure
  3. WatchWatch the video (32 min) on Section 2.7: Hypothesis Tests

    The video stops before the end of the section (during the two-sided test); read the remainder of the section in the course notes. Again starting from the studentised estimator we develop hypothesis tests related to a single \(\beta\)-parameter. I start here from the assumption that you are familiar with the concept of hypothesis tests (null and alternative hypothesis, null distribution, one-sided and two-sided tests, p-values). If not, then I suggest to first study the relevant parts of the course Concepts of Probability and Statistics. It is important that you can formulate the conclusions of a hypothesis test very precisely, keeping in mind that (1) a non-significant result does not prove that the null hypothesis is true (non-significance = inconclusive = insufficient evidence that the opposite is true); (2) the p-value gives information about the strength of the conclusion. I also want to stress that hypothesis tests form only one tool in the statistician's toolbox! Parameter estimation in combination with confidence intervals is often more informative.

Comparing two groups, and causation

Goal: use a dummy regressor to compare two groups, and know when an effect has a causal interpretation.

  1. ReadRead Section 2.9: Binary Dummy Regressors

    There is no video recording available. In this section you learn how a dummy variable can be defined and used as a regressor so that the regression model can be used for statistical inference for the comparison of two groups (e.g. two treatments). The t-test for testing \(H_0: \beta_1=0\) in this model is equivalent to the two-sample t-test! In later sections (Chapter 5) dummies will be used for the formulation of ANOVA models as regression models.

  2. DoMake Exercise: Smoking
  3. ReadRead Section 2.10: Association versus Causation

    There is no video recording available. Here you learn the very basics of Causal Inference, which is a rather modern topic in statistical science. It aims to estimate effect size parameters that have a causal interpretation. Studying causality is made possible through the introduction of the concept of counterfactuals or potential outcomes (Section 2.10.2). In randomised studies (Section 2.10.3) it is very straightforward to estimate causal effect sizes; it becomes much less simple when we move to observational studies (this will be the topic of Section 4.1).

Estimating the mean, predicting, and explained variability

Goal: distinguish the confidence interval for the conditional mean from the prediction interval, and interpret \(R^2\) with care.

  1. WatchWatch the video (15 min) on Section 2.11: Estimation of the Conditional Mean Outcome

    This section focuses on the estimation of the conditional mean \(E(Y\mid x)\), i.e. for a given value of the regressor, say \(x\), what is the estimate of the average outcome. This estimate is of course the corresponding point on the fitted regression line. You also learn how to construct a 95% confidence interval for this conditional mean.

  2. WatchWatch the video (29 min) on Section 2.12: Predictions and Prediction Intervals (PI)

    Here we look for a prediction of the outcome \(Y\) for a given value of the regressor (predictor) \(x\). You will learn that this is numerically equivalent to the estimate of \(E(Y\mid x)\), but it has another interpretation, and it comes with another interval: the prediction interval, which is conceptually different from the confidence interval of \(E(Y\mid x)\). This is very important to see.

  3. DoMake Exercise: Galton’s data
  4. WatchWatch the video (19 min) on Section 2.13: Decomposition of the Total Sum of Squares

    In this section we see how SSTot can be decomposed into SSR and SSE, i.e. SSTot = SSR + SSE. This tells us that the total observed variability in the outcome variable is composed of variability that can be explained by the regression relationship (SSR) and unexplained variability (SSE). You also learn about the coefficient of determination, \(R^2\). It's important to understand that \(R^2\) should not be over-interpreted.

  5. DoMake Exercise: \(R^2\) and prediction

    This exercise serves to make you understand that \(R^2\) should not be over-interpreted.
