From one regressor to several

Goal: formulate the additive multiple linear regression model and interpret a parameter as an effect "while the other regressors remain constant".

  1. WatchWatch the video (10 min) on Example (Lead concentration)

    We start again from an example: children living near a lead smelter, with the finger-wrist tapping score (FWT) as outcome. First the data are explored with two simple linear regression models. Note the counter-intuitive result: the longer the children lived near the smelter, the better their average score seems to be. Keep this result in mind; it will be explained later in this chapter.

  2. WatchWatch the video (26 min) on Section 3.1: The Additive Multiple Linear Regression Model

    Here the simple linear regression model is extended to a model with more than one regressor. The good news is that, thanks to the matrix notation, all theory of Chapter 2 remains valid: least squares estimation, sampling distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and prediction intervals. Only the number of columns of the design matrix \(X\) changes, and the degrees of freedom become \(n-p\). What does change is the interpretation of the parameters: \(\beta_j\) is the effect of regressor \(x_j\) on the mean outcome while the other regressors in the model remain constant. This phrase is extremely important. It is why we speak of an effect corrected for (or controlling for) the other regressors.

  3. DoMake Exercise: Lead concentration

    Make the exercise yourself; the solution is in the course notes. You fit a model with age and exposure, you interpret both parameters, and you make an informative graph. Pay attention to the exact wording of your conclusions: "for children of the same age, ...". Also look at the graph in the solution: it shows what additivity means (parallel lines).

  4. AIAI and the words "corrected for"

    This step continues the exercise that you have just made (Exercise: Lead concentration). Give the R output of your model with Age and Exposed to the AI assistant and ask for a conclusion about the effect of exposure, in two sentences, for a non-statistician. Check two things: (1) Does the conclusion say that the effect holds for children of the same age, or is the correction for age lost in the translation? (2) Does it say that exposure causes a lower score? The data come from an observational study, so recall Section 2.10. Improve the text together with the AI until you would be willing to sign it.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Write down the additive model with \(p-1\) regressors, both for a single observation and in matrix notation. What are the dimensions of \(X\) and of \(\beta\)?
    • Show, starting from the conditional mean, that \(\beta_1\) is the change in the mean outcome when \(x_1\) increases with one unit while the other regressors remain constant.
    • Which results of Chapter 2 can be used without any change, and where does \(n-2\) become \(n-p\)?
    • In the lead example, the effect of the blood lead concentration is "corrected for Totyrs". Explain in your own words what this means, without using a formula.
    • Why can you say nothing about causation in the lead concentration example?

Interaction

Goal: understand the non-additive model, interpret an interaction effect as effect modification, and test effects in the right order.

  1. WatchWatch the video (20 min) on Section 3.2: The Non-Additive Multiple Linear Regression Model

    An interaction term \(\beta_3 x_1 x_2\) is added to the model. The consequence is that the effect of \(x_1\) becomes \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2\): it depends on the value of the other regressor. This has an important implication: in a model with interaction the main effects \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) no longer have a meaningful interpretation on their own, and testing them makes no sense. This leads to hierarchical model building: first test for the interaction effect, and look at the main effects only if there is no indication of interaction (and then after removing the interaction term and refitting the model). For estimation nothing new is needed: the product \(x_1 x_2\) is simply a third regressor. The lead concentration example shows a non-significant interaction, and it also shows that the sign of a main effect estimate in a model with interaction can be misleading.

  2. WatchWatch the video (12 min) on Example (Blood Pressure)

    The blood pressure example, now with dose, gender and their interaction. Here the interaction is significant: the effect of the dose is different for men and women. This is why interaction is also called effect modification: gender modifies the effect of the dose.

  3. DoMake Exercise: Blood Pressure

    You are asked for the standard error and the confidence interval of the dose effect for women. This effect is the sum of two parameters, \(\beta_1+\beta_3\), which can be written as \(c^t\beta\). You will need the covariance matrix of \(\hat\beta\) from Chapter 2. This technique (inference about a linear combination of parameters) will come back several times in this course, so make sure that you can do it in R.

  4. WatchWatch the video (8 min) on Example (Lead concentration)

    Back to the lead concentration example, now with age added to the model. Watch what happens to the effect of the number of years living near the smelter: it is no longer significant. This is the explanation of the counter-intuitive result from the start of the chapter: older children have lived there longer, and older children have a better score. The interpretation of a parameter always depends on which other regressors are in the model. The video ends with a very short look at Section 3.3.

  5. DoMake Exercise: Lead concentration

    With three regressors there are three two-way interactions and one three-way interaction. Apply the hierarchical approach: start with the highest order interaction.

  6. AIAI as assistant for a linear combination

    From this chapter on you let the AI assistant help with parts of an analysis, while you stay in charge. First finish the blood pressure exercise yourself (Exercise: Blood Pressure). Then describe the fitted model to the AI assistant (regressors dose, gender coded 0 = man and 1 = woman, and their interaction) and ask it for R code that gives the estimate, the standard error and the 95% confidence interval of the dose effect for women. Before you run it, predict what the code must contain: which vector \(c\), which covariance matrix, which degrees of freedom? Then run it and compare with your own result and with the solution in the notes (SE = 0.25, interval from 1.7 to 2.7). Important: if the AI used a package or a function that you do not know, ask it to do the same with matrix calculations only.

  7. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • In the model with interaction, what is the effect of \(x_1\) on the mean outcome? And what is the interpretation of \(\beta_1\) on its own?
    • Why should you not test \(H_0: \beta_1=0\) in a model that contains the interaction \(x_1x_2\)?

Multicollinearity

Goal: recognise multicollinearity, understand why it inflates the variance of the parameter estimators, quantify its effect it with the variance inflation factor and know some solutions to this problem.

  1. WatchWatch the video (18 min) on Section 3.4: Multicollinearity

    The lead concentrations of 1972 and 1973 are both related to the outcome, but when both are in the model one of them is far from significant and the standard errors are larger. This is the problem of multicollinearity: strong correlation between regressors makes the parameter estimators imprecise, which gives wider confidence intervals and less power. The simulation study of Section 3.4.1 demonstrates this: the larger the correlation between the regressors, the larger the variances of the estimators. The recording stops during this simulation study, so read the end of Section 3.4.1 in the course notes, and look in particular at the three-dimensional plots (Figures 3.3 and 3.4): with strongly correlated regressors it is not clear where the plane should be.

  2. DoMake Excercise: repeatedly fitting model with \(\rho=0.99\)

    Again a simulation study, with the concepts and the algorithm that you know from Chapter 2. Seeing the fitted plane jump around from one repeated experiment to the next is the best way to understand what imprecise estimation means.

  3. ReadRead Section 3.4.2: Variance Inflation Factor

    There is no video recording available. Here you learn about the variance inflation factor (VIF): the variance of \(\hat\beta_j\) is written as the variance without multicollinearity multiplied by \(1/(1-R_j^2)\), where \(R_j^2\) comes from a regression of \(x_j\) on the other regressors. The results are given without proof. Note the warning at the end: thresholds such as 5 or 10 exist, but never apply a strict threshold. Also read the example, in which the VIFs of the lead concentration model are computed in R.

  4. AIExplaining a p-value that changes

    In the notes, the effect of Ld72 is significant in a simple regression model (\(p=0.015\)) but not at all in the model that also contains Ld73 (\(p=0.88\)). Fit the three models of the example of Section 3.4 yourself, give the three outputs to the AI assistant, and ask it to explain this change to a researcher who concludes that "Ld72 has no effect after all". Does the explanation mention the correlation between the regressors and the larger standard errors? Does it also mention that the interpretation of the parameter is different in the two models? Then ask what the researcher should do, and compare the advice with Section 3.4.2 and with what you know about non-significant results from Section 2.7.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • What is multicollinearity, and what are its consequences for standard errors, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests?
    • In the simulation study, what happens to the variances of \(\hat\beta_1\) and \(\hat\beta_2\), and to the correlation between them, when the correlation between the regressors goes from 0 to 0.99?
    • Which matrix shows why correlated regressors lead to large variances?
    • What is \(R_j^2\), and what do the extreme values 0 and 1 mean?
    • A VIF equals 4. What does this tell you about the standard error of that parameter estimator?
    • Does multicollinearity make the least squares estimator biased?
    • Does multicollinearity invalidate the inference?

Outliers, leverage and the model assumptions

Goal: identify influential observations, assess the assumptions of a multiple linear regression model, and know what can be done when they are violated.

  1. ReadRead Section 3.5: Leverage

    There is no video recording available. The fitted values can be written as \(\hat{Y} = HY\), with \(H\) the hat matrix, so that each prediction is a weighted mean of all outcomes. The leverage \(h_{ii}\) measures the influence of observation \(i\) on the fit, and the leverages have mean \(p/n\). An outlier with a large leverage is an influential outlier. Keep in mind the role of the statistician: outliers are not removed just because they are outliers; they are reported, so that the scientists can check what happened.

  2. ReadRead Section 3.6: Assessment of the Model Assumptions and Remedial Measures

    There is no video recording available. All methods of Section 2.8 can still be used, with residual plots for each regressor separately or with the predicted outcomes on the horizontal axis. Leverage and multicollinearity are now also part of the assessment. In the bacterial count example the linearity assumption is clearly violated, and several remedial measures are tried. Two points deserve your attention. (1) Assess the assumptions in the right order: as long as the model for the mean does not fit, the plots for the variance and for normality cannot be trusted. (2) Transforming the outcome changes the interpretation of the parameters, because \(E(\log Y) \neq \log E(Y)\). As for Section 2.8: the methods are simple, but interpreting the plots requires practice.

  3. DoMake Exercise: Lead concentration, Exercise: Blood pressure

    Assess the model assumptions for two models with an interaction term. Write down your own assessment of each plot before you look at the solution. In the first exercise you will find large VIFs; make sure that you understand why this often happens in models with an interaction term.

  4. AITesting the AI on a transformed outcome

    Tell the AI assistant that you have fitted the model \(\log Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i\) and ask how \(\beta_1\), or \(\exp(\beta_1)\), should be interpreted in terms of the mean of the outcome \(Y\). Compare the answer carefully with the paragraph on remedial measures in Section 3.6. Many texts state that \(\exp(\beta_1)\) is the ratio of the mean outcomes at \(x+1\) and at \(x\); the notes explain why this is wrong. If the AI makes this claim, confront it with the argument of the notes and see how it reacts. If it does not, ask it under which conditions the claim would be approximately or exactly true for another summary of the outcome distribution, and note what you cannot verify with the course notes.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • What is the hat matrix, and why can a prediction be called a weighted mean of the outcomes?
    • What is the leverage of an observation, and with which value do you compare it?
    • When is an outlier a problem, and what should a data scientist do with it?
    • Why may you make a residual plot with the predicted outcomes on the horizontal axis?
    • In the bacterial count example, why should you not yet trust the plot for the constant variance assumption in the first model?
    • Name three remedial measures for a violation of the linearity assumption. Which of them keeps the interpretation of the parameters in terms of the mean outcome?
    • A model contains \(x\) and \(x^2\). Is it still a linear regression model? Why?

Model selection

Goal: know the selection strategies, understand why selection based on hypothesis tests should be avoided, and select a prediction model with cross validation.

  1. ReadRead Section 3.7: Model Selection

    There is no video recording available. First you learn forward selection, backward elimination and stepwise selection based on hypothesis tests. These methods are still very popular, but the section gives a list of arguments why they should no longer be applied; study these arguments well, because you will meet these methods in practice. The key question is always: what is the research question? If the goal is to assess the effect of a particular regressor, model selection is not the way to go, since the interpretation of the target parameter depends on the other regressors in the model. If the goal is prediction, regressors are selected with a criterion that estimates the prediction error, such as leave-one-out cross validation (CV), which can be computed from the leverages without refitting the model \(n\) times. Advanced model selection in the context of prediction modelling is outside the scope of this course.

  2. DoMake Exercise: Lead concentration

    Apply forward selection with CV as the selection criterion, and interpret the CV of the final model on the scale of the outcome.

  3. AIAI as assistant for model selection, and looking back

    First make the exercise yourself. Then use the AI assistant in two ways. (1) Ask an open question: "I want to know whether the blood lead concentration has an effect on the finger-wrist tapping score. I also have age, sex, IQ and years living near the smelter. How should I choose my model?" Does the AI propose stepwise selection or the removal of non-significant regressors? Compare its advice with the arguments in Section 3.7. (2) Ask it to write R code for forward selection with the leave-one-out CV criterion for the lead concentration data, and check the result against your own: the same selected regressors, and a CV of the final model of about 90.6. Finally, read through your AI logbook for this chapter. In Chapter 2 the AI explained and you checked; in this chapter it also did parts of the work. Which checks caught a problem? Write down what you will always verify yourself.

  4. AIAI as a discussion partner for discussing methods for which even statisticians don't agree

    Related to the previous model selection problem. Discuss with AI why model selection methods based on p-values are appropriate / non-appropriate. Reflect on this discussion with AI.

  5. CheckCheck yourself

    You can move on when you can answer these questions.

    • Describe forward selection, backward elimination and stepwise selection.
    • Give at least four arguments against model selection based on hypothesis tests.
    • You want to assess the effect of one specific regressor. Why is stepwise selection a bad idea, and what should guide the choice of the other regressors?
    • What is the expected test error, and how does leave-one-out cross validation estimate it?
    • Why is no refitting needed to compute the CV criterion in a linear model?
    • Why is the CV of the finally selected model still too optimistic?
